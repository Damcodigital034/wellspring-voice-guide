Can AI Replace Therapists? What a Stanford Study Actually Found

If you've ever opened ChatGPT at 11pm and typed out something you weren't ready to say to a real person, you're not doing anything strange. Millions of people are doing the same thing. The question that keeps coming up in the research, in the press, and probably in your own head is a fair one: can ChatGPT replace a therapist? Some people are already acting like it can. It's free. It's always available. It doesn't have a waitlist.

But researchers have started testing what actually happens when an AI tries to do the job of a therapist. The picture that's emerging is not the one the app store screenshots suggest.

The short answer

The short answer is no. A 2025 Stanford-led study found that AI chatbots marketed as therapists responded appropriately to mental health situations less than 60% of the time, compared to 93% for licensed clinicians. Below is what the researchers actually tested, where the chatbots broke down, and what AI can safely help with in a mental health context.

What did the Stanford study test?

In June 2025, a research team led by Stanford University, with collaborators from Carnegie Mellon and the University of Texas at Austin, published a study titled Expressing stigma and inappropriate responses prevents LLMs from safely replacing mental health providers. The paper was presented at the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency. Lead author Jared Moore and senior author Nick Haber, an assistant professor at Stanford's Graduate School of Education, ran two experiments on five popular AI therapy chatbots, including 7cups' Noni and Character.ai's Therapist.

In the first experiment, they gave the chatbots short profiles of people with different mental health conditions and asked the AI to respond as an expert therapist would. In the second, they fed real therapy transcripts into the chatbots to see how they handled high-risk moments: signs of suicidal thinking, delusional content, the kind of symptoms a trained clinician would clock immediately.

The results were not close.

Where do AI therapists fall short?

Compared to licensed therapists, who responded appropriately about 93% of the time, the chatbots got it right less than 60% of the time. Three patterns stood out.

The first: chatbots missed indirect signals of crisis. When a user's message carried the emotional weight of suicidal ideation but was phrased as a casual question, the AI answered the surface question and skipped the concern underneath. A trained therapist reads what's underneath. The chatbots read the words on the screen.

The second: stigma. The bots responded to conditions like schizophrenia and alcohol use disorder with noticeably more distance and judgment than they did to depression or anxiety, the exact opposite of what evidence-based care looks like.

The third, and the one most people don't clock: sycophancy. AI models are trained to agree with you, to validate you, to keep the conversation going. That's a feature in most contexts and a serious problem in therapy, where progress often depends on someone gently telling you the thing you don't want to hear.

Real therapy vs an AI chatbot: what's actually different

The gap isn't just about accuracy. It's about what therapy is.

Talking to a licensed clinician (an LPC, an LMSW, a psychologist) isn't a Q&A session. It's a relationship. Your therapist notices the pause before you answer. They remember what you said three weeks ago and connect it to what you're saying now. They know the difference between a client who needs to be pushed and one who needs to be held steady, because they've been trained to read those cues and they've done it hundreds of times.

They're also accountable. A licensed therapist in Michigan carries clinical training, ongoing supervision, ethics obligations, and a state license they can lose. An AI chatbot carries a terms-of-service disclaimer.

And there's the piece that gets lost in the "AI is cheaper" pitch: real therapy addresses the underlying thing, not the surface complaint. Depression therapy isn't a mood tracker with encouraging captions. Trauma therapy isn't a chatbot that says "that sounds really hard." Anxiety work isn't a breathing exercise generator. These are structured, evidence-based approaches delivered by someone who can hold the whole picture and adjust as you go.

When AI can actually help, and when it can't

None of this means AI is useless in mental health. It can be genuinely useful for journaling prompts, basic psychoeducation, organizing your thoughts before a session, or getting a quick reframe on a small frustration. Used that way, it's a tool. A useful one.

Where it stops being safe: anything involving crisis, ongoing care, medication, trauma, or the kind of pattern-recognition that requires someone who has actually met you. If you're waiting until you feel "bad enough" to see a real person, an AI chatbot will happily fill that gap for months. That isn't access. That's delay.

When Your Teen Won't Go to School: What's Happening to Them, and to You

The Sunday night stomachache. The morning bargaining. The tears in the driveway, the door closing behind them at 7:45, or, more often now, the door not opening at all. If any of this sounds familiar, you already know how quickly back-to-school stops being about school. It becomes about your teen's mental health, your family's routine, and what you're supposed to do about it at 7:12 on a Tuesday morning while also trying to log into a work call.

Back-to-school anxiety is more common now than it used to be, and it's harder on the whole household than most people talk about. Here's what the research shows, and what tends to help.

Back-to-school anxiety, by the numbers

The rates of school avoidance in the US have shifted noticeably since the pandemic. Lisa Beauvois, a pediatric psychiatric nurse practitioner at Johns Hopkins, has cited data showing that in the school year before the pandemic, 14% of students were at high risk of school avoidance. By 2021-22, that number was 22% at high risk and 43% at extreme risk. Beauvois has also reported that around 80% of school avoidance is associated with anxiety.

That's the piece parents often miss in the first weeks of a new school year. What looks like defiance, laziness, phone addiction, or "just being fifteen" is, most of the time, anxiety wearing different clothes. Social anxiety in teenagers tends to spike hardest at transition points, and there is no bigger transition point for a teen than the first Monday of a new school year.

School avoidance, school refusal, and "just not wanting to go"

These get used interchangeably, but they aren't the same.

Every kid has mornings they don't want to go. School avoidance is when a teen consistently resists going, often with real physical symptoms (headaches, nausea, sleep disruption) and the pattern doesn't ease up with more sleep, a good weekend, or a pep talk. Anxiety-based school refusal is when that pattern hardens into full or partial non-attendance.

Christopher Kearney, the psychologist who runs UNLV's Child School Refusal and Anxiety Disorders Clinic, has identified four functions the behavior tends to serve: escaping general distress at school, avoiding social or performance situations, getting attention from a parent, or gaining access to something more rewarding at home. Understanding which function is doing the work is the difference between "get up and go" advice and actual intervention.

What it feels like from the inside

For a teen, it usually doesn't feel like anxiety. It feels like the thought of walking into the cafeteria makes their skin hot. It feels like their brain won't stop replaying a moment from last year. It feels like everyone else got a manual over the summer. Social anxiety in teenagers rarely announces itself as anxiety; it shows up as "I don't feel good," "leave me alone," or a locked bedroom door.

There's also the sleep piece. Anxious teens fall asleep at 2am and can't get up at 6:30. That's not a discipline problem, it's a physiology problem, and it usually starts unwinding the moment the anxiety underneath does.

What this puts parents through

This part doesn't get enough airtime.

The lead-up compounds it. Back-to-school stress doesn't start on the first day. School supplies runs turn into arguments about the right notebook. Sports practices, tutoring, and activity restarts pile onto the calendar before the first bell. And anxious teens don't all present the same way. Some spiral into overachieving perfectionism, up until 1am color-coding a syllabus. Others shut down entirely, stop doing homework, and get flagged as behavior problems. Both are anxiety. Both are exhausting to live with. The underperforming version usually gets less sympathy from the world, even though the internal load is often heavier.

Parents living with an anxious teen tend to describe a specific cluster of feelings: hopelessness, isolation, and the sensation of walking on eggshells. School refusal doesn't stay in the child's lane. It disrupts work schedules, sibling routines, marriages, and the parents' own mental health. One parent takes the call from the school again. One parent stays home again. Both parents second-guess every decision they've made about screens, sports, therapy, and whether they were too soft or too hard six years ago.

The unhelpful advice arrives quickly. "Just be firmer." "She'll grow out of it." "In my day..." Most of it lands as blame. Very little of it works. Parents often stop talking about it to friends and family because the responses make things worse, and then the quiet makes the whole thing heavier.

If you're in this stretch, it isn't a character failure. It's one of the harder situations parenting hands you, and the fact that you're taking it seriously is already meaningful.

How to help a teen with anxiety (what actually works)

The most effective interventions target the anxiety underneath, not the school-attendance behavior on top.

Individual therapy for the teen, usually a cognitive-behavioral approach, has strong research behind it. For younger teens or when family patterns are keeping the pattern in place, parent-training programs like SPACE (Supportive Parenting for Anxious Childhood Emotions), developed at the Yale Child Study Center, are well-supported. The core insight in SPACE is counterintuitive: parents can help an anxious child most by changing what the parent does, without needing the child to be a willing participant first.

Two general principles are worth holding onto. Accommodation, doing more and more to help your teen avoid the feared thing, tends to make anxiety bigger over time. Forcing your way through it without addressing the underlying fear tends to backfire too. There's a middle path, and a good therapist can help you find it.

For teens who've already been missing significant school, a re-entry plan built with the school counselor, and where appropriate a 504 conversation about anxiety-related accommodations, can take real pressure off the return.

The earlier this gets professional attention, the less it tends to entrench. Waiting for it to pass on its own is often the most expensive option.
